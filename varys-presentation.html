What we built,
and what kept
breaking.

Varys began as a browser extension for recording test cases. Three months later it is two different kinds of test, and knowing which one to reach for is most of what those three months bought.

By Mothil V, with Samarth · 12 June – 22 September 2026

Definition

Varys tells you when the app stopped looking like it did the last time someone said it was right.

Four words. Every one of them turned out to be harder than it sounds, and each of the next five slides is one of them fighting back.

  • 01RecordSomebody walks the app once — clicking, typing, naming the screens worth watching.
  • 02ApproveA human looks at the pictures it took and says that is correct. Only then is anything being watched.
  • 03RerunWhenever you want, against whichever environment you want — on a button, or on a schedule.
  • 04CompareEvery new picture against the approved one, and anything that moved goes in front of a person.

Why we started

Checking how the app looks is the work everybody skips.

It is slow, it is boring, and it is the first thing dropped when a release is due. So it doesn't happen — and a broken chart, a collapsed layout, or a KPI quietly showing the wrong number ships, and a customer finds it.

The bet

If catching that could be made cheap enough — cheap to write, cheap to run, cheap to read — it would stop being the thing that gets skipped.

Everything that follows is the cost of the word cheap.

Challenge one · 12 June

01

Recording a test is cheap. Recording forty is not.

The first answer was a browser extension. You click through your app and it writes down every step and every screen worth watching. In three days the whole loop worked end to end.

What it cost
  • Twenty careful minutesPer test, and it has to be careful — a mis-click is a step you didn't want.
  • The scarcest person you haveOnly someone who knows what right looks like can record it.
  • Every single timeNot hard. Just costly, repeatedly, forever.

A tool whose adoption cost is a scarce person's afternoon does not get adopted.

Challenge two · 16 June · Varys MCP

02

So describe the test in a sentence, and let Claude walk it.

Claude connects to Varys, is handed a real browser, and drives it from a plain-English brief — reading the page, clicking, typing, naming checkpoints.

The important part

Claude does not write a script that runs later. It drives the app once, and Varys writes down what it did using exactly the same code the human recorder uses. What comes out is an ordinary test — you cannot tell it apart from a hand-recorded one, and no AI is involved in running it ever again.

  • Minutes, from a sentenceInstead of a careful half hour.
  • It lands as a draftAI-written tests sit in a review queue until a human promotes them. Claude cannot put a test into the release gate on its own.

Challenge three · 23 July · Contextual comparison

03

Pixels have no opinion about what matters.

A softened edge, a chart that animates in one frame later, a timestamp that is supposed to change — all read as failure in exactly the same way a genuinely broken layout does.

A suite that cries wolf gets ignored, then deleted.
Two answers, both shipped
  • iMasks and sensitivityPaint over the region that always changes; set how much movement counts. Adjustable right on the failing run, re-checked instantly.
  • iiContextual comparisonFor screens where the same cannot be defined in pixels at all, the two pictures are judged on whether they mean the same thing — with the reasoning written down beside them.

And one rule that came with it: if the judgement can't be made at all, the run says so. A model outage never reads as green.

Challenge four · 27 August · Repair mode

04

Then the app moves, and the test is pointing at something that isn't there.

A button gets renamed, a layout gets reorganised. The run goes red for a reason that is not a bug — and fixing it by hand means re-recording the whole journey.

Repair mode

You read the failure and ask your Claude to fix it, naming the run. Varys re-drives the test to the exact step that broke — against the page that broke it, not the app as it stands today — and parks there. Claude looks at the real page, re-points the step, and the fix is written onto the test.

Nobody needs a test repaired at 3am. The failure is still there in the morning, and the person who reads it is the person who knows whether the app changed on purpose.

Challenge five · 16 September · Agent-driven tests

05

Some journeys refuse to be written down as steps at all.

The page takes a different amount of time on every run. A control is there only sometimes. The route through the app legitimately differs. Repair can't help: there is no single correct step to repair it to.

A test with no steps

Just a list of checkpoints — how to reach each one, in plain English, and what should be true when you get there. Your own Claude re-walks the whole thing on every run, on your machine, on your subscription, and reaches each state however it judges best.

The obvious risk is an agent that quietly checks less and still reports pass. That is handled by machinery, not by asking politely:

  • The checkpoint list is a closed set it must fill — a name outside it is refused.
  • Every slot starts red. A slot never reached stays red, even if the agent crashes or says nothing.
  • It cannot approve a baseline, and it cannot set its own verdict.
  • There is a wall-clock limit it cannot talk its way out of.

Where it actually landed

The last one did not replace the first. We ended up with two tracks, on purpose.

Three months did not end with one of these winning. It ended with us knowing which is which — and that is the thing that cannot be bought quickly.

The most common red that isn't a bug

“Locator not found” is five different problems wearing one error message.

  • 01The element never rendered — because the app failedAn API returned a 500, or never answered. The button is missing because the data is. The most common cause — and not a locator problem at all. That's the test doing its job.
  • 02It rendered lateVarys waits, patiently, up to thirty seconds. A streaming answer or a slow chart can take longer than that.
  • 03It's there twiceThirteen identical delete buttons. Two score within a hair of each other, so Varys refuses to guess — and says not found rather than clicking the wrong one.
  • 04The signals rottedClass names that change every build, ids generated fresh every page load, a label with yesterday's date in it. The element is there; nothing about it still matches what we wrote down.
  • 05It's there, but unreachableHidden until you hover. Inside an embedded frame. Scrolled out of a long list that only renders what's on screen.

And what each one led to

  • 01Every run now records the API calls it sawFailures and slow successes, attached to the step that was running. You open the red run and see the 500 that explains the missing button — instead of spending an hour on selectors.
  • 02Waits that understand a page that's still thinkingHold until the page actually stops changing, rather than until a fixed timer expires — up to two minutes for a streaming answer.
  • 03Refusing to guess is the fixAmbiguity is reported as ambiguity and never acted on. The scoring was retuned so that a unique parent can break a tie between thirteen identical siblings.
  • 04No fix — this one is a property of the worldSignals decay because apps get rebuilt. This is exactly why repair mode exists.
  • 05Reveal on hover, descend into frames, respect what's visibleAnd for a screenshot only, fall back to the recorded path — marked on that run so you know it happened. A click never falls back: a wrong screenshot is cheap, a wrong click is not.

Three of the five were engineering problems, and they were engineered. Two are properties of the world — and they are the reason the second half of this timeline exists.

Act two

So there's a
release next
week.

Six steps. This is what I'm proposing we actually do — not a description of what already happens.

Step one

01

Build a suite.

A suite is a saved group of tests you want to run together — say, everything that matters for a release. You fill it two ways, and you can mix them:

  • By folderEverything in it — including anything filed there later. A standing selection, so a suite keeps up with itself.
  • By individual testFor the handful that don't fit a folder.

One suite per area, or one per release. Suites are free — make several. Only promoted, live tests go in.

Step two

02

Run it against the release.

  • One pressAgainst one environment, or several at once — staging and production in a single go.
  • It runs in the backgroundThree tests at a time. Nobody sits and watches; the page updates itself.
  • Or don't press anythingGive the suite a schedule and it runs nightly, or every hour, in whichever timezone you mean.
  • Slack says when it's doneWhich is usually how you'll find out.

Step three

03

Read one combined report.

Not forty results. One, with four possible outcomes — and they mean genuinely different things.

  • PassedEvery picture matched what was approved.
  • ChangedSomething looks different. Might be intended, might be a bug — that's step four.
  • FailedThe test couldn't get where it was going. Nothing was compared.
  • Needs reviewNothing was compared, and that's not a failure either — a first run in a new environment has nothing to compare against yet.

Deliberately: if a comparison could not be made at all, it never reads as a pass.

Step four · this is how you update

04

Decide, one screen at a time.

Side by side, overlay, swipe, or blink between the two — whichever makes the difference obvious.

  • ApproveThe new look is correct. It becomes the baseline from now on. Permanent — the old one is replaced and does not come back — so it asks you to confirm, and records who did it and when.
  • RejectIt's a bug. File it.
  • Approve allWhen a whole screen legitimately changed, one press takes the lot.
  • There is no bulk rejectOn purpose. Accepting a redesign wholesale is routine; throwing away a run's worth of evidence in one click is not.

Getting false alarms from a region that always changes? Adjust the mask and the sensitivity right there on the run, and re-check instantly.

Step five

05

When a test couldn't get there at all.

  • iCheck the API panel firstBefore anything else. The button may be missing because the backend is. That is a real finding, and no amount of repairing the test will help.
  • iiIf the app genuinely moved, ask Claude to repair itName the run. It re-drives to the broken step, looks at the page that actually broke, re-points the test and writes the fix in place.
  • iiiRerun to prove itA repair isn't finished because Claude says so. It's finished when the run is green.

Step six

06

And the journeys the suite can't hold.

Agent-driven tests do not join suites and cannot be scheduled. Nothing can run one unattended — that is the design, not a gap.

  • Run them by hand before the releaseFrom Varys, or by asking your own Claude directly. Ten minutes of someone's attention, on the two or three journeys worth that attention.
  • Their baselines are approved from the review queueNo run needed just to produce a picture — the pictures from authoring are the pictures.
  • They still can't grade themselvesA human approves what correct means. Always.

Before you ask

Five things Varys deliberately will not do.

  • 01It won't repair anything unattendedNo 3am auto-fix. The person who reads the failure is the person who knows whether the app changed on purpose.
  • 02It keeps no version history, and nothing rolls backA test has exactly one definition. The past lives on the runs — each one keeps a copy of what it walked, as evidence. Nothing can be restored from it.
  • 03Agent-driven tests never join the release gateThey cost your subscription and take minutes. A gate has to be cheap and unattended; they are neither.
  • 04Every environment approves its own baselinesA new environment starts blind, on purpose. Correct on staging is not a claim about production.
  • 05Varys never pays for the AIAuthoring, repair and agent runs all happen on the asker's own Claude. Which is also why none of them can happen without a person asking.

What I'm asking for

Four things,
this week.

  • 01Pick your areaThe screens you'd be embarrassed to ship broken.
  • 02Get two or three journeys inRecord them, or describe them to Claude and let it write them. Fifteen minutes either way.

  • 04Bring back everything it got wrongEvery false red is a bug in Varys, not in you. That list is the most valuable thing anyone can hand me right now.
